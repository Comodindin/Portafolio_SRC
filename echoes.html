Case study, interactive exhibition

Echoes of the Past, Voices of the Present

An interactive exhibition about the path Mexican women take to political power: the obstacles along the way, from gender roles to media violence, and the women who opened the road.

My role
Research and Unreal Engine programming. I also programmed the first prototype in Unity.
Team
Saraí Rosas Chávez, Giulianna Mercado Zilli, Irene Fernández del Castillo, Ángela Ximena Andrade Méndez, Sofía Carmen Villette Cordier Carvajal
Context
ASE III, Universidad Iberoamericana Puebla
For
Young Mexican women, students and teenagers
Built with
Unreal Engine (first version in Unity)

The problem

In Mexico, women who enter politics face structural barriers and systematic violence, and the media constantly scrutinise and discredit them. Even with the parity law, many are judged on their looks, their personal life or supposed shortcomings, while their achievements are overshadowed.

That media narrative reinforces gender stereotypes and discourages women from public life, keeping democracy unequal.

Our answer: an exhibition that walks visitors through that path, so they feel the obstacles and leave with a realistic but inspiring view of what's been achieved.

Research

The project started with field research in January 2024: five interviews with three women interested in feminism and politics and two political scientists, plus a review of press and feminist articles.

5 interviews: 3 users and 2 experts

2 participatory methods: concept mapping and card sorting

What we found

  • Higher expectations Participants linked women candidates with progress, social justice and equity, and men candidates with corruption, abuse of power and fear.
  • Feminism vs. patriarchy In card sorting, patriarchy grouped with oppression, gender roles and impunity; feminism with rights, participation, equality and freedom.
  • Having candidates isn't enough Equality still feels far off: structural and social change is needed, not just women on the ballot.
  • The cost of being accepted Experts noted that women in politics feel pushed to act “masculine” to be accepted, and that quotas can become a facade without real change.

Who it's for

In this stage the profiles were redefined from new insights. We widened the audience to younger visitors and included men, to build awareness and empathy. Three main groups:

  • Young Mexican women who want leadership roles or a voice in politics
  • Students of political science, law and communication
  • Teenagers between 14 and 18
AM

Adriana Martínez

23, philosophy and literature student from Oaxaca

“The country's social and political outlook interests me a lot, but it overwhelms me that there hasn't been real progress in recent years.”
Need
Safe, respectful spaces to speak freely, and to see laws that protect women actually enforced.
Holding her back
Her voice isn't heard, and her conservative family doesn't let her speak up for what she believes.
JT

Javier Torres

18, political science student from Jalisco

“It's not about who holds power, but how they use it to make real change.”
Need
Spaces to share ideas and learn other perspectives, and critical thinking to spot gender bias around him.
Holding him back
He notices bias but doesn't always know how to question it, and doesn't feel he knows enough to debate.

Each persona came from real interviews. User testing helped refine the design and pointed to key areas, like the media's role in shaping women politicians' public image.

Design principles

Each principle answers a specific insight from the research. The exhibition is progressive: it shows how gender barriers build up from childhood to political life, so the problem is understood little by little.

  • Easy to navigate
  • Information in a natural, logical order
  • Always keep everything visible and at hand
  • Simple, clear language
  • A sense of community
  • Aesthetics and minimalism
  • Safety, privacy and trust
  • Give the user control

The rooms

Nine themed spaces, each with an interactive challenge you have to solve to open the next door. Every challenge stands for a real obstacle.

  1. Kitchen

    Find the keys

    The domestic role imposed on women. Hidden keys, symbols of knowledge, unlock a box with a notebook and pencil: education as the road to autonomy.

  2. TV room

    Change the channels

    How TV repeats gender stereotypes from childhood. You flip channels until a positive message lets you through.

  3. Library

    Find the books

    Misogynistic messages set against inspiring quotes: the obstacles and the support women find in their education.

  4. Poster hallway

    Find the candidate

    Women made invisible in politics. To move on, you peel off men's posters covering a woman candidate.

  5. Chamber of Deputies

    Press the switches

    A hostile setting with cameras everywhere and critical murmurs. Getting out takes several buttons: a support network between women in power.

  6. Newspaper hallway

    Read the papers

    Media violence through defamation and rumours, alongside examples of media that help change the story.

  7. Timeline tunnel

    Press the button to hear them

    It starts in the dark and leads to a lit space with a timeline of feminism and stories from women in politics.

  8. Hall of fame

    Press the buttons

    Mexican women who opened the way, and a wall where visitors can add an achievement of their own.

  9. The great hall

    Step into a capsule

    Gender equality laws written by women and men, reflection capsules with personal questions, a graffiti wall for messages and a booklet to take home.

Ten front pages designed for room 6. Every headline is a real form of media violence against women in politics: comments on their looks, rumours about their private life, doubts about their ability.

Inside the exhibition

The rooms as they ended up in Unreal Engine. The negative rooms are dark and heavy; the path opens up into light and pastel colours towards the end.

Flow

The main path is linear, so every visitor gets the same essential information. Only at the end does it branch into optional choices that make the experience personal without breaking the story.

  1. Start
  2. Kitchen keys and door
  3. Change the TV channel 3 times
  4. Pick up the library books
  5. Find the candidate's poster
  6. Switch off the cameras
  7. Collect the newspapers
  8. Press the timeline buttons
  9. Press the hall of fame buttons
  10. Add an achievement to the wall? optional
  11. Enter the great hall
  12. Answer the capsule questions? optional
  13. Assemble the puzzle
  14. End

We also placed every room on an interaction continuum, from passive to interactive, across feedback, control, co-creativity, productivity, communication and adaptivity. Compared with the first version, key spaces now invite co-creation and communication, and every interactive element has a clear purpose.

Visual identity

Colour palette

Two palettes follow the emotional tone of each space: soft, warm and cheerful colours for the positive rooms, and deeper, heavier ones for the negative rooms, to carry tension and discomfort.

Typeface

Coolvetica, modern and legible, for titles and information. Chopin Light, more decorative, for accents and symbolic elements.

Assets

A large set of illustrated portraits of Mexican women from different eras, plus the 3D models for every room.
